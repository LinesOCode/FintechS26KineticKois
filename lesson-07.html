Lesson 07: Bonds & Fixed Income

A bond is essentially a loan you make to a government or corporation. When you buy a bond, you're lending money to the issuer (the borrower) for a fixed period, and the issuer promises to pay you regular interest payments (called the coupon) and return your principal at the end of the bond's life (called maturity). This is fundamentally different from owning stock (which represents ownership) versus lending money (which bonds represent). For example, a 10-year U.S. Treasury bond might pay 4% annually. If you invest $1,000, you receive $40 each year for 10 years, then get your $1,000 back at the end. The advantage of bonds compared to stocks is predictability—you know exactly how much you'll receive and when you'll receive it. The disadvantage is that potential returns are lower than stocks historically, and you're exposed to inflation risk (if inflation rises, your fixed payments become worth less).

There are different types of bonds, each with different risk and return characteristics. Government bonds (like U.S. Treasuries) are considered safest because they're backed by the government's ability to collect taxes and, in the U.S. case, essentially unlimited money printing power. They offer lower interest rates because they're lower-risk. Corporate bonds are issued by companies and typically offer higher interest rates to compensate for higher risk—if the company fails, you may not get your money back. Municipal bonds are issued by local governments and often offer tax advantages. High-yield bonds (also called "junk bonds") are issued by companies with poor credit ratings and offer very high interest rates to compensate for high default risk. Understanding these categories helps you build an appropriate bond allocation—someone near retirement might prefer mostly government and high-quality corporate bonds, while someone younger can afford more junk bonds for higher yield.

Bond prices have an inverse relationship with interest rates, which confuses many beginning investors. If you buy a bond paying 4% and interest rates rise to 5%, new bonds pay 5%, so your 4% bond becomes less attractive. If someone wants to buy your old bond, you have to sell it at a discount (lower price) to make it competitive with the new 5% bonds. Conversely, if interest rates fall to 3%, your 4% bond becomes more valuable, and you can sell it at a premium (higher price). This is why bond prices fluctuate based on interest rate movements, even though the bond itself will still pay you the promised interest and return your principal if held to maturity. For long-term investors who plan to hold bonds until maturity, these price fluctuations don't matter—you'll get the promised payment regardless. But for those who might need to sell before maturity, interest rate risk is real.

The role of bonds in a diversified portfolio is crucial for stabilization. Historically, when stock markets crash, bond prices often rise (because investors flee to safety and drive up bond prices). This negative correlation means a portfolio with both stocks and bonds is more stable than one with only stocks. A 60/40 portfolio (60% stocks, 40% bonds) during the 2008 financial crisis fell about 20%, while an 80/20 portfolio fell about 30%, and a 100% stock portfolio fell about 55%. While 20% is still a significant loss, it's far preferable to 55%. Bonds essentially act as a shock absorber, smoothing out volatility and helping you stick to your long-term investment plan during market turmoil.

For young investors, bonds typically play a smaller role because time is on their side and they can afford to endure stock market volatility. A 25-year-old might have 90% stocks and 10% bonds. For mid-career investors, bonds become more important—perhaps 60% stocks and 40% bonds. For near-retirees, bonds might dominate—perhaps 40% stocks and 60% bonds. The specific allocation depends on individual risk tolerance and timeline, but the principle remains: bonds provide lower, more predictable returns with lower volatility, making them the perfect complement to stocks in a diversified portfolio. Understanding bonds and their role in portfolio construction is essential to being a well-informed investor.

Key Takeaways

  • Bonds are loans you make to governments or corporations
  • You receive regular interest payments and return of principal at maturity
  • Different bond types carry different levels of risk and return
  • Bond prices inversely correlate with interest rates
  • Bonds stabilize portfolios and act as shock absorbers during crashes
