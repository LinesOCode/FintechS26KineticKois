Lesson 01: What is a Stock?

A stock is a share of ownership in a company. When you buy a stock, you're essentially purchasing a small piece of that business. Think of a company as being divided into thousands or millions of equal pieces called "shares." Each share represents a fractional ownership stake in the company. For example, if a company issues one million shares and you own one share, you technically own 1/1,000,000th of that company. This ownership gives you certain rights, including the right to vote on major company decisions at shareholder meetings and potentially receive a portion of company profits if they're distributed as dividends.

Stocks are bought and sold on stock exchanges, which are electronic marketplaces where buyers and sellers come together. The most famous stock exchanges include the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) and the NASDAQ. When you buy a stock, the price you pay is determined by supply and demand—if many people want to buy a stock, its price goes up, and if many people want to sell, the price goes down. The price reflects what investors believe the company is worth at any given moment, based on factors like the company's financial performance, growth prospects, competitive advantages, and overall economic conditions.

There are two primary ways to make money from stock ownership: capital appreciation and dividends. Capital appreciation occurs when the stock price increases over time, allowing you to sell your shares for more than you paid. Dividends are portions of company profits paid directly to shareholders, usually quarterly. Not all companies pay dividends; some reinvest all profits into growing the business. Young, fast-growing companies often prioritize expansion over dividend payments, while more established companies may pay regular dividends to reward patient investors.

Understanding stock ownership also means understanding that stocks represent ownership in real businesses with real employees, products, and operations. When you invest in a company's stock, you're making a bet on its future success. This is fundamentally different from lending money (as with bonds) because as an owner, your potential upside is unlimited—if the company does exceptionally well, your shares could become worth much more. However, there's also downside risk: if the company performs poorly, your shares could lose value, and in worst-case scenarios like bankruptcy, you could lose your entire investment.

Stocks also come in different types. Common stocks are what most people think of when they hear "stocks"—they provide ownership and voting rights. Preferred stocks are a hybrid between stocks and bonds, offering fixed dividend payments and higher priority if the company goes bankrupt, but often without voting rights. Understanding these distinctions helps you make informed investment decisions as you build your portfolio and grow your wealth over time.

Key Takeaways

  • A stock represents fractional ownership in a company
  • Stock prices are determined by supply and demand in the market
  • You can profit through capital appreciation (price increases) and dividends
  • Stock ownership gives you voting rights and a claim on company profits
  • Different stock types offer different rights and benefits
